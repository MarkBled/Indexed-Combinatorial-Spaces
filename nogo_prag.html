No-go konflikti v razporeditvi študentov in prag gruče

Koliko razporeditev študentov v sobe po 4 ostane veljavnih, ko dodamo pravila „ta dva ne smeta biti skupaj“? In ali se to da izračunati brez preštevanja vseh razporeditev? Poskus na 12 do 200 študentih pokaže, da je odgovor odvisen predvsem od strukture konfliktov, ne le od njihovega števila.

3/(n−1)verjetnost, da sta dva izbrana študenta v isti sobi po 4
≈ 10305vseh razporeditev 200 študentov v 50 oštevilčenih sob
k ≈ n/2prag, kjer se naključni konflikti zlijejo v veliko gručo in točno štetje postane eksponentno

1. No-go razporeditev ni naključna

Pri 12 študentih (3 sobe, 34 650 razporeditev) lahko vse indekse narišemo kot mrežo: vrstica je izbira sobe 1, stolpec izbira sobe 2. Rdeča polja kršijo vsaj eno pravilo.

  • En konflikt: no-go indeksi so pravokotni bloki, njihovo število je točno N·3/(n−1) = 9 450 (preverjeno za tri različne pare).
  • Več konfliktov: vzorec ostane determinističen (unija blokov), a se z vsakim novim konfliktom razdrobi.

2. Točno štetje brez preštevanja

Število veljavnih razporeditev da formula vključitev in izključitev po podmnožicah S konfliktov:

veljavni = ΣS ⊆ konflikti (−1)|S| · N(S)

N(S) je število razporeditev, v katerih so vsi pari iz S skupaj. Ključna ugotovitev: N(S) je odvisen le od profila blokov (koliko parov, trojic in četveric mora biti v isti sobi), zato ga izračunamo z zaprto formulo. Konflikte nato razdelimo na ločene gruče in vsako obdelamo posebej. Podmnožice, kjer bi moralo biti v isti sobi več kot 4 študentov, odpadejo takoj.

Cena zato ni 2k (k = število konfliktov), ampak je odvisna od velikosti največje gruče. Vse vrednosti so preverjene s preštevanjem in z neodvisnim dinamičnim programiranjem pri 12, 16 in 20 študentih.

3. Primerjava struktur pri 200 študentih

Disjunktni pari, skupine po 3, veriga in naključni konflikti dajo skoraj enake deleže, zato se njihove črte prekrivajo s približkom. Odstopa le zvezda. Pri naključnih konfliktih so prikazane le točke, kjer je bil točen izračun izvedljiv.

Zvezda (en študent v konfliktu z mnogimi) je poučen primer: pri 150 konfliktih je veljavnih le 1,4 %, ker mora ta študent dobiti sostanovalce izmed preostalih 49. Preprost približek (1 − 3/(n−1))k, ki predpostavlja neodvisne konflikte, tukaj zgreši za faktor 7.

4. Prag: nastanek velike gruče

največja gruča (povprečje 8 naključnih poskusov)prag k = n/2 = 100

To je znan pojav iz teorije naključnih grafov (Erdős in Rényi, 1960). Dokler je konfliktov manj kot približno polovica študentov, so gruče majhne (do nekaj deset študentov) in točno štetje traja sekunde. Okoli k ≈ n/2 se gruče nenadoma zlijejo v eno, ki pri 200 konfliktih zajame že okoli 160 študentov. Takrat mora metoda pregledati podmnožice ene ogromne gruče in izračun postane eksponenten.

V realnih primerih, kot je razporejanje v hostel, so konflikti običajno lokalni. Takrat sta točno število veljavnih rešitev in indeks samo veljavnih rešitev dosegljiva. Težko postane, ko konflikti tvorijo gosto povezano mrežo. To ne pove ničesar novega o vprašanju P proti NP, lepo pa pokaže, kje v konkretnem problemu nastopi meja med lahkim in težkim.

No-go conflicts in student room assignment and the cluster threshold

How many assignments of students to rooms of four remain valid once we add rules such as “these two must not share a room”? And can this be computed without counting all assignments? Experiments with 12 to 200 students show that the answer depends mainly on the structure of the conflicts, not only on their number.

3/(n−1)probability that two given students end up in the same room of four
≈ 10305all assignments of 200 students to 50 numbered rooms
k ≈ n/2threshold at which random conflicts merge into one large cluster and exact counting becomes exponential

1. The no-go distribution is not random

With 12 students (3 rooms, 34,650 assignments) all indices can be drawn as a grid: the row is the choice for room 1, the column the choice for room 2. Red cells violate at least one rule.

  • One conflict: the no-go indices form rectangular blocks, and their number is exactly N·3/(n−1) = 9,450 (checked for three different pairs).
  • Several conflicts: the pattern stays deterministic (a union of blocks) but fragments with every new conflict.

2. Exact counting without enumeration

The number of valid assignments follows from inclusion–exclusion over subsets S of the conflicts:

valid = ΣS ⊆ conflicts (−1)|S| · N(S)

N(S) is the number of assignments in which all pairs in S share a room. The key observation: N(S) depends only on the block profile (how many pairs, triples and quadruples must share a room), so it has a closed formula. The conflicts are then split into separate clusters, each handled on its own. Subsets that would force more than 4 students into one room drop out immediately.

The cost is therefore not 2k (k = number of conflicts) but depends on the size of the largest cluster. All values were checked against enumeration and an independent dynamic program for 12, 16 and 20 students.

3. Comparing structures with 200 students

Disjoint pairs, groups of 3, a chain and random conflicts give almost identical shares, so their lines overlap the approximation. Only the star deviates. For random conflicts only the points where an exact computation was feasible are shown.

The star (one student in conflict with many) is instructive: with 150 conflicts only 1.4 % of assignments are valid, because that student must find roommates among the remaining 49. The simple approximation (1 − 3/(n−1))k, which assumes independent conflicts, is off by a factor of 7 here.

4. The threshold: a large cluster appears

largest cluster (mean of 8 random trials)threshold k = n/2 = 100

This is a known phenomenon from random graph theory (Erdős and Rényi, 1960). While there are fewer conflicts than about half the number of students, clusters stay small (up to a few dozen students) and exact counting takes seconds. Around k ≈ n/2 the clusters suddenly merge into one; at 200 conflicts it already contains about 160 students. The method then has to go through subsets of one huge cluster, and the computation becomes exponential.

In real cases such as hostel scheduling, conflicts are usually local. Then the exact number of valid solutions, and an index over valid solutions only, are within reach. It becomes hard when conflicts form a densely connected network. This says nothing new about P versus NP, but it shows clearly where the boundary between easy and hard lies in a concrete problem.
